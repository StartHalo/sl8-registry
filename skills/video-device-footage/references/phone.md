# Phone bystander writer

SL8 wording of the Everygen caught-on-a-phone prompt writer (`everygen/shotoniphone-92f669`, no
licence stated, restated and cited; the source named a phone brand, which is not used here). It writes
one paragraph and its `Duration: Ns` line; SKILL.md generates. Approach step numbers are in
brackets. `$F` is the skill's `scripts/footage.mjs`.

## The one test and the model

The tie-breaker for any choice: would a startled person fumbling their phone have got this, or a film
crew? Go with the fumble. If the clip is astonishing but reads as generated, it has failed, so realism
wins every conflict. Shake, exposure surges, focus searching, glare, rolling-shutter jelly and wind
overloading the mic all hide where the model slips; request that kind of imperfection. The loudest
proof of a real person is their reaction: the picture lurching past the subject because they flinched,
a gasp right against the mic, the scramble to find the thing again. Smooth narration, a gliding camera
or a tidy, pretty frame kill the effect.

What the model does (text to video, with sound made alongside):
- Whatever you pin, it keeps; whatever you leave open, it invents. Pin vantage, event, light and
  anything that must hold.
- Busy fast action in the subject smears into mush; one slow, focused action survives. So split the
  motion: the handset jitters and lurches throughout, while the event itself is a single physical
  happening with clear cause and effect, given one plain verb.
- Keep it far: onlookers shoot from where they stand, and a middle-to-long view starves the model of
  the close detail it gets wrong. Reach toward distant things with a grainy, wobbly digital zoom rather
  than placing the lens beside them.
- Faces break down when they stay on screen: the person filming is never shown; anyone else is far,
  turned away, in motion or a silhouette; a natural event or an animal is usually the star.
- Writing, interface elements, signs and logos melt into nonsense: forbid them and stage scenes with
  none.
- Braces `{like this}` mark words to be voiced; the speaker stays out of shot.
- Speech moves at roughly two words a second: about 15 words fit 8 s, 20 is the limit.
- There is no negative field: the bans live together in the final line; the phrase "no music" holds,
  "no background music" does not.

## Situation [3]

A single person filming, one location, one astonishing thing, one payoff; you could say it in a
sentence and read it from a thumbnail.

| The request gives | Do this |
|---|---|
| A whole scenario | Keep it; identify its single highest moment and plan backwards from that frame |
| A subject | Choose the safest spectacle for it that people would share, and land on its peak |
| A vantage (boat, car, porch) | Keep it; pick the mode, light and event that suit that spot |
| A time or weather | Keep it; adjust the light and the low-light behaviour of the phone |
| A reaction line | Shape the moment so that exclamation becomes the payoff |
| Nothing, or a mood | Make up something new; never repeat a known example; vary subject, vantage, light and line |

Ideas, each with the frame the clip exists for:
- Weather extremes: a distant waterspout or twister, peak when the column reaches the surface and
  kicks up spray or dust; surf smashing over a harbour wall, peak when white water erupts and runs at
  the filmer, who backs off; a shelf cloud advancing, peak when the blue sky vanishes and the light
  falls; lightning close by, peak on the flash, a pause, then thunder hitting the mic; huge hailstones,
  peak when one shatters on impact or a burst of them leaps off a pool; a desert dust wall, peak when
  it engulfs the filmer and daylight goes dark orange; bizarre clouds, peak when the view tips upward
  into them.
- Animals: a humpback breaching near a small boat, peak on the leap and the spray drenching everyone;
  a bear ambling past on a trail, peak when it comes alongside, enormous and unconcerned; a moose
  strolling a town street, peak on one passing glance toward the lens; a moose shedding an antler,
  peak on the pop and the antler landing in snow; dolphins or a shark close to shore, peak as one sweeps
  under the lens; a starling cloud folding at dusk, peak as it dives low and roars past; an osprey
  hitting the water, peak as it rises with a fish and water pouring off.
- Sky and wonder: a launch plume at dusk opening into a vast glowing spiral above the roofs; an aurora
  surging across the whole sky; a fireball breaking apart; surf glowing electric blue at night; an
  enormous moon rising over a hill.
- Good timing: a family dashing in out of sudden rain with a child squealing; a phone or ball caught in
  mid-air; a small kindness seen from across the street; a pet losing its mind with joy at a return.
  People far away, moving, unnamed.
- Surreal scale: a cruise liner gliding past the end of a lane or along a village canal; a migration
  or swarm crossing a road; fog spilling down a ridge in slow motion like water; the payoff is something everyday in front of something that cannot be.

## Safe lane and casting [4]

- Fake phone clips mistaken for real have triggered false storm warnings and fake disaster stories,
  and the AI label falls off when they are reposted. Wonder, joy and mild comedy are both the safe
  lane and the shareable one; every person and animal is fine at the end.
- Invented throughout: nobody real or named, no celebrities, no real towns or landmarks, no brands,
  logos, plates or news, and never a re-created event.
- Danger is light and passing: the frightening thing is far away or already leaving; people retreat
  laughing; the clip finishes on wonder, not harm.
- Refuse quietly and redirect, even when asked: injury, drowning, crushing, death; a crash or collapse
  with people inside; a believable disaster with casualties; violence, weapons, crime against someone;
  self-harm; animals killing, dying or suffering graphically; sexual content; children in real danger.
  Move to the closest safe spectacle and keep the format.
- The person filming stays invisible: a thumb or fingertip at the edge, a hand pointing, the rim of a
  case at most; their voice is their presence. Use the rear camera; switch to the front camera only
  when a person's or group's reaction is the point, and keep those faces in motion, an arm's length away.
- Onlookers are nameless, distant and mostly facing away, with one plain clothing tag if any.

## Duration [5]

The fewest seconds that land the peak and the reaction; round up when torn, since cutting off the
payoff is worse than a little extra.

| Size | Length | Beats | Paragraph |
|---|---|---|---|
| A jolt or a short pass (a strike and its thunder, an antler falling, one breaking wave, a bird grabbing a fish, an animal wandering past) | 5-6 s | 2-3 (hold, then the peak and gasp) | about 2,000-2,500 characters |
| A standard catch (a quiet or searching hold, the approach, the peak, the reaction) | 7-9 s | 3-4 | about 2,300-2,800 |
| Long wonder (something that develops and moves: a spout drifting toward the beach, a plume spreading, a set of waves, a liner sliding by, a long aurora surge) | 10-12 s | 4-5, longer build | about 2,500-3,000 |

Opener and closer together use roughly 1,500 characters, so beats rather than characters set the
length. Slow developments and travel need seconds more than words; a silent held moment with one
whisper can be brief; a near-miss with screaming and scrambling needs an extra beat. Bands are a
guide; any whole second that fits. The source's examples were 6, 8, 11 and 12 s.

## Camera [6]

The clip is the file the phone itself recorded, seen from inside: no handset, no hand or arm holding
it, no person filming, ever. Describe camera movement as what "the view" or "the frame" does ("the
view jerks up past it and swings back", "the frame tips skyward and burns out"). Writing "the phone"
as a moving thing on screen is how models end up drawing a hand with a fake camera screen and a red
dot. (The source's own worked examples slip on this; don't imitate them.) Declare the point of view
first and forbid the handset in shot in opener and closer.

Choose a mode from the vantage:
- Reactive (default): held one-handed, yanked up and swung to catch the event, overshooting then
  correcting, shaking with every breath and flinch, always re-centring late. Use it for anything
  sudden or moving; overshooting and pulling back is what sells it.
- Braced: two hands, held as still as possible but still drifting and quivering, often aimed upward,
  sometimes creeping into a grainy, very shaky digital zoom. For slow wonders (a plume, an aurora, a
  big moon, a far funnel).
- Through a window: a windscreen, house window, screen door or plane window, with smudges, dust and
  interior reflections drifting across, and a wiper, sill or dashboard in the foreground. Good for
  weather seen from a car or an animal at the door; it hides faces and simplifies physics.
- On the move: the frame bouncing and jolting with each step as the filmer hurries toward or alongside
  the event.

Every mode: a little wide-angle lens, slightly bent at the corners and nowhere near fisheye; everything
in focus with no film-style blur; constant small tremors from breathing and heartbeat; auto-exposure
that flares when aimed at bright sky and sinks into black in shade, catching up late; autofocus that
goes soft and then clicks sharp when the view jerks or something rushes in; rolling-shutter skew and
wobble on quick swings and vibration, worst when zoomed; light noise and blocky compression; real glare, a smudge glowing or a veil of flare near a bright light; a stray thumb, finger or hair at an edge.
A single take throughout.

Never: a cut; a crane, orbit, dolly, glide, drone move or stabiliser smoothness; a slick cinematic
push in or out (a jittery digital zoom is fine; a polished optical one is not); rack focus or bokeh; slow
motion; any angle the person couldn't have had. No direction words (none of: we see, close-up, angle
on, cut to, zoom in on, pan across). Missing the peak by half a second and swinging back to it beats a
perfect catch.

## Beats [7]

Number them ("Beat one: ..."): in one take, order is choreography. The duller the start, the harder
the spectacle lands.
1. Start: either a beat of nothing much (still water, an ordinary sky, an empty road), which says "I
   was only filming by chance", or already reacting (the view mid-swing, breath already ragged),
   which says urgency. Make the vantage clear at once.
2. Build: the thing appears, grows or approaches, described by material, motion and state. The view
   reacts (lurches, overshoots, loses focus, surges in brightness) while the thing does one coherent
   action. The authentic sound sits here: wind on the mic, a held breath, half a word.
3. Peak: one clean physical event with a plain cause (a whale rising and falling back reads real; a
   string of ten events does not).
4. Reaction and out: the gasp tops out, spray arrives, the filmer staggers or laughs, the view loses
   the subject and recovers it. Stop on the peak or half a second later; never on quiet aftermath.

Writing: keep the two motions separate (the view lurching, overshooting, searching and burning out is
background texture; the event gets one clean verb); reactions trail off and break, never full
sentences; keep people low-key (the filmer breathes, murmurs, swears under their breath; a shout from
nearby is shredded by wind; nobody performs); one vivid thing, and around it something plain and a bit
ugly (a dented hull, wet sand, snowed-in cars, a power cable).

## Realism and light [8]

- Movement driven by breath and pulse, not a float; the reactive swing overshoots and corrects; tipping up to the sky swings and smears everything; zooming multiplies the shake and the jelly. Smooth
  motion is the clearest generated tell.
- Name one or two auto-everything delays per clip (brightness flaring and settling late, colour
  balance shifting after the light changes, focus searching); they do a lot.
- Physics settles: spray falls back, a wave runs up and drains away, an animal's weight displaces water
  and earth, hail bounces and scatters; each motion has its visible cause one clause earlier; nothing
  floats, jumps or passes through.
- Swarms, flocks, crowds and particles: the model's default is an even, flat speckle that looks like a
  filter. Ask for countless individual elements (each bird beating its wings, each person separate),
  density varying from dense clumps to thin wisps, and fluid motion (pouring, folding, a ripple running
  through as thousands turn), plus the veto in frames.json `phone/swarm-veto`.
- No face held sharp and still in the centre; let shake, distance and the event take the eye.
- Light and what gives it away:
  - Overcast (safest): flat, cool and drained, the sky going white when the view tips up.
  - Strong sun: hard contrast, clipped highlights, dark shadows, flare and ghost shapes near the sun,
    shimmer far off.
  - Dusk: warm and low, long shadows, a blue-to-peach sky, grain starting to creep in.
  - Night or twilight: almost dark, heavy noise crawling in shadow, lights flaring and smearing (a lamp,
    the moon, the glow of a launch), brightness pumping, blacks crushed; noisy and soft, never clean.
  - Through glass: room reflections gliding across, dust and smudges, slight softness and ghosting, a
    wiper or sill in front.
- Weather hitting the lens: raindrops beading and running, spray smearing, a gust knocking the frame
  sideways, a corner misting over. Instantly reads as outdoors.

## Speech and audio [9]

Sound is half the realism or more and the place fakes most often slip: it has to sound like the handset's microphone picked it up live, never mixed. Keep it sparse.
- The filmer's reaction: a single broken fragment around the peak, close to the mic, breathless,
  cracking, too loud, unfinished, in braces with a gender and register tag so the voice suits an
  unseen person (for example "a man's voice, breaking, right against the mic"). Shapes to rotate, never
  repeated across clips: the same stunned phrase twice; a disbelieving question that breaks off; a
  warning to the person next to them; a stream of exclamations. Optionally a flat call from a companion, or a bystander's cry ripped apart by the wind, nothing else. About 15 words, 20 at the outside;
  silence is useful and a gasp with no words reads real.
- The Audio block, in event order, opening with "Audio:":
  1. Ambience: wind battering the phone's mic (the most authentic outdoor sound; use it whenever the
     sky or weather is involved), waves, rain, a silent road, an indoor heater, far-off traffic, a
     distant dog.
  2. The filmer: shaky breathing near the mic that hitches and races when the shock comes, a quick intake of breath, feet slipping or scrambling on gravel, snow or sand, fingers knocking the mic. 3. What the spectacle itself sounds like from where the filmer stands: a breach's wet thump and roar, lightning's crack
     and delayed thunder, hail shattering, a wave hissing and crashing, a funnel's low rumble; for huge
     silent things (a plume, an aurora, a murmuration) almost nothing, which is itself convincing.
  4. The reaction line.
- Two moves: wind overload as a feature (phone mics clip and cut out in wind; phrase in frames.json
  `phone/wind`), which also covers the model's audio flaws; and the almost-silent wonder clip (a soft
  breeze, one distant dog, a single whisper), often the strongest.
- Finish with a natural mono mix, a little distorted, then "no music".

## Assembly [10]

One flowing paragraph, present tense, no names, about 3,000 characters at most, five sections:
1. Opener (`node $F frame phone`, row `phone/opener`) with its camera slot (the mode, written as motion
   of the view or frame) and light slot. Near verbatim allows slot text and small changes (a twilight
   clip can drop the sharp-throughout phrase and add grain). Its opening words state that this is the
   recording itself and ban the handset in shot (lead phrase: row `phone/opener-lead`).
2. "Scene geography:" first: where it is, how it is lit, the plain foreground, far-off onlookers facing away,
   and where the spectacle is and how far.
3. The numbered beats, motions kept apart, physics in the verbs.
4. "Audio:" with the tagged line in place, the reaction last, then "no music".
5. Closer (row `phone/closer`).

`Duration: Ns` on the next line. Clips exported from a phone never contain the camera app's interface,
so a clean frame is entirely authentic: no recording dot, timer, battery, zoom bar, focus square,
timestamp or brand caption, and no legible signs in the scene. Captions belong to a later edit.

## Final checks and traps [11]

Checks: only the paragraph and the tag; the length lets the peak and the reaction land; one filmer, place, spectacle and payoff, ending in wonder; what we watch is the file the phone itself saved, with no handset, hand
or arm in frame and the opener saying so; the view lurches, overshoots and searches (or braced, through
glass, on the move), deep focus, one take, no cuts, slow motion, shallow focus or cinematic moves, and
motion written as the view or frame; one clean event while the handheld carries the chaos; the
spectacle at a believable distance; faces far, turned away or never shown; opener and closer intact
with the text and interface bans in both; colour flat, cool and uncorrected, never milky, hazy or
graded; "Scene geography:" and "Audio:" present; sparse captured audio with one broken tagged line,
"no music", near-silence for vast quiet sights; all invented and safe.

Traps: a steady, floating or stabilised picture (the biggest giveaway); a view from outside showing a handset or the hand gripping it (the commonest failure); film-style lenses, grading, neat composition or slow
motion; a camera that never reacts; any writing or interface; a sharp centred face; a melting
fast-moving subject; the lens planted right on the spectacle; a filmer who narrates or performs;
everything too clean, neat and lovely; candy or HDR colour; music; anything forbidden; a repeated line,
spectacle or framing; length or setting words in the paragraph.

## Variation [16]

Vary the subject family (weather, animal, sky, kindness, near-miss, the surreal), the mode, the time
and light, the vantage (a beach, a boat, a car, a porch, a street, a trail, a window, a roof), the kind
of peak (joining, crashing, bursting, blooming, striking, passing), and the line and its voice.
Identical dialogue across look-alike clips is a known tell. Only opener, closer and section labels
repeat.
