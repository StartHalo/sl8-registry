# Method: April Dunford's positioning, in her order

From April Dunford's quickstart guide (https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning).
Work through the parts in this order; each comes from the one before.

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What best-fit customers would do if the product did not exist:
   often a spreadsheet, doing it by hand, hiring someone, building it in-house, a general tool, or a
   named competitor. List only what buyers actually consider; avoid "phantom competitors".
2. **Unique attributes.** What the product has or does that those alternatives do not: features,
   integrations, how it is delivered, how it is priced. Only what it can deliver today; a planned
   feature is labelled `(planned)`.
3. **Value.** What each attribute lets the customer do, in their terms: hours saved, a risk removed,
   money kept. Each value has proof: a figure with its unit and date, a case study, a review, or
   the request. With no proof, say so.
4. **Best-fit customers.** Who cares most about that value, as segments a founder can list by name:
   - company type and size (a sub-vertical and a headcount band);
   - the buyer and the user (say when they are the same person);
   - the trigger: an observable, datable event that sends them looking;
   - where to reach them: a named forum, directory, marketplace, newsletter, event or search phrase;
   - the evidence, with its instrument and n.
   Name the primary segment and why.
5. **Market category.** The frame that makes the value obvious to those customers. Prefer a
   category buyers already search for, and say why it beats the nearest alternative frame.

Then the statement (Geoffrey Moore's form): "For <best-fit customers> who <situation>, <product> is
a <category> that <value>, unlike <main alternative>, because <unique attribute>."

**Weak positioning shows** as buyers needing an extra call to understand what the product is. The
founder tests it in the next five sales conversations: do buyers repeat it back?
