# Positioning in five parts (April Dunford)

Work through the parts in this order; each one comes from the one before. Use S1's evidence and
the buyers' own words.

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What best-fit customers would do if the product didn't exist:
   often a spreadsheet, paper, a general tool, or doing nothing. Avoid "phantom competitors" that
   buyers never actually consider.
2. **Unique attributes.** What the product has or does that those alternatives don't: features,
   integrations, how it's delivered, who's behind it. Only what the product can actually deliver
   today.
3. **Value.** What those attributes let the customer do, in their terms: time saved, risk removed,
   money made or kept. Prove each one with a fact from S1 (a review, a figure, a case study).
4. **Best-fit customers.** Who cares most about that value, specifically enough that the founder
   could list ten of them.
5. **Market category.** The frame that makes the value obvious to those customers. Prefer a
   category buyers already search for; a new category needs a lot of explaining.

Then the statement: "For <best-fit customers> who <situation>, <product> is the <category> that
<value>, unlike <main alternative>, because <unique attribute>."

**Weak positioning shows** as buyers needing an extra call to understand what the product is.
Note in `## Open decisions` how the founder can test it: the pitch in the next five sales
conversations, and whether buyers repeat it back.

## The messaging sheet

From the positioning, for anyone who writes for the product (the founder, a contractor):

- **One-line pitch:** the statement, said plainly in under 20 words.
- **Three messages,** one per value, each with its proof (a figure, a quote, a customer).
- **Objections and answers:** the three most likely, from reviews, S1 or the founder's notes
  ("we already use a spreadsheet", "too expensive", "will staff use it?").
- **Words to use and avoid:** buyers' own words for the problem, and jargon to drop.
