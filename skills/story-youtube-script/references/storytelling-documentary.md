## Intake

Fill gaps with the defaults below and name them in the delivery (HR12: no question tool).

1. **The story.** Real events, checked facts, named sources. Anything not confirmed carries a
   `[VERIFY: ...]` tag for the customer; a documentary never invents a fact.
2. **Narrator voice.** Choose one and hold it:
   - **Companion in the room:** first person, contractions, reactions left in, seams visible; for
     investigations, challenges and host-led documentaries.
   - **Unseen narrator:** third person, absent until the verdict, plain but formal; for mysteries,
     procedurals and company sagas.
   Default: the unseen narrator; a kit or brief may name the other.
3. **Lane** (one of eight): a game or challenge; a scale ladder (size or geography); an economic
   argument; a cinematic manhunt or procedural; a forensic mystery; an investigative essay; a
   company saga; a comic retelling.
4. **Length.** About 145 spoken words a minute; long documentaries run 30-80 minutes. Default: 30
   minutes. At 4 minutes or less, use Short-form mode.
5. **What is held back.** The thing the viewer does not get until 70-85 percent. If the title
   gives away the outcome, hold back how it happened.

## Hooks

Three options, each 30 s or less and 40-80 words, from different rows. Within the first 30 s, say
the title's promise again in close to the same words, with the premise on screen.

| Type | Shape (our wording) |
|---|---|
| Prize and preview | The host has assembled something extreme; the person who meets a condition takes a prize; a bigger tease follows. |
| Dated scene | An exact date and place; someone doing something ordinary, told in the present; one detail that is wrong. |
| Shadow of the ending | Tacked onto a scene: the people in it cannot know that ruin or reversal is on its way. This loop spans the whole video. |
| Misconception reversal | What the viewer believes, its opposite, and a comparison showing how much bigger, worse or stranger the truth is. |
| Record object | The biggest or strangest of its kind, two figures each with a comparison, then the question of how it works, fails or escapes. |
| Investigator's obsession | Months spent on one question, and the usual account of it is wrong or partial. |
| Archive collage | Real clips or audio first; the narrator enters over them with the framing claim. |
| Broken explanation | The accepted answer stated, then shown not to hold. |

## Structure: the cold open as a loan

The opening borrows against the middle, which repays it.
- **0-2:** the hook.
- **2-8:** framing and the promise: why it matters, an offer to show how it happened, and up-front
  credit to sources in investigative lanes.
- **8-15:** jump back to the beginning; the chronology starts.
- **25-35:** the story levels off for the first time, which makes an easy act break: deliver part of the payoff, then re-hook with a
  line that raises the stakes.
- **35-70:** three to five chapters that escalate. Each ends unsettled, never resolved. Reveal in
  stages (who, then how, then where). One twist or interrupt a chapter.
- **70-85:** the payoff zone: return to the opening scene now that the viewer understands it; the
  largest reveal sits here.
- **85-100:** a verdict with its hedge → widen to a general truth or a closing maxim. Finish a few seconds after the emotional peak; nothing essay-like follows.

**Lane variants:**
- *Game or challenge:* explain the rules like a sports commentator, a twist each act, counters as
  the spine, finish as the winner reacts.
- *Scale ladder:* a single question, a climb through sizes or distances, a comparison per rung,
  and a humbling final note.
- *Economic argument:* claim → the numbers that prove it → background → how it works → the best
  objection → a closing maxim; this lane does not need a cold open.
- *Company saga:* a short "why this touches your life" essay (5-10 percent) before the chronology.
- *Comic retelling:* a farce that keeps escalating, a joke every 5-15 s; an in-character narrator
  is allowed (never a real person's persona).

## Register and retention

- **Tense works like a camera:** tell past scenes in the present to put the viewer inside them;
  use the past for analysis.
- Alternate short punches (4-8 words) with explaining sentences of 20-30 words, and do not let two
  long sentences sit together.
- **No bare numbers:** every figure gets an object to compare it with. Every term gets a quick
  gloss in the line; every person gets a role tag the first times they appear.
- Plant explicit promises ("we'll come back to that") in act one and keep every one. Only the opening scene's loop may run past 5 minutes unanswered.
- Call back only to setups this script planted; never "remember that detail" without the earlier
  line.
- After each partial payoff, repeat the core question in the same words, about three times a video.
- Give the viewer a counter to track (people left, money, depth, dates).
- Stretch single reveals: read a name a letter at a time; stop to ask "did you catch that?".
- **Hedge at the peak:** the biggest claim carries one honest caveat ("we could not confirm
  this"). Keeping trust keeps viewers.
- When exposition passes about 60 s, break the pattern: a stunt, a skit, a street interview, a
  puzzle.
- Payoff rhythm by lane: ladders every 10-20 s; challenges every 20-40 s; statistic-led essays every
  30-60 s; procedurals pay off a chapter each 2-4 minutes, with big reveals near 45, 65 and 80 percent.

## Ending

No invented sponsor, brand, merchandise or plug. A specific integration the user asks for sits at
a clean boundary, never inside a rising sequence. Otherwise: hedged verdict → general truth or
closing maxim → stop seconds after the emotional peak. One plain final line (a next video or a
subscribe invitation) is optional and always last; ending cold on the maxim is a valid signature
for mysteries.

## Anti-patterns

- Warm-up, "before we start", or a jingle intro.
- Early verdicts: opinion lives only in the last 10-15 percent and is marked as opinion.
- Overclaiming: point out the weak spots of your own account.
- Recap padding: refer back to a loop; never replay it.
- Flat chapter transitions: every chapter ends unsettled.
- Moralising in the middle: ethical framing goes once at the start or in the coda.
- Invented dialogue or inner thoughts presented as fact.

## Short-form mode

At 4 minutes or less, drop the long-form map; keep the register, tense as camera, no bare numbers
and the ethics.
- About 150 words a minute: 3 minutes is about 420-470 spoken words; each sentence must earn its
  place.
- The opening sentence is the hook (a dated scene or a record object); skip the framing section,
  no separate credibility beat (fold sourcing into a clause).
- A single loop: the detail the opening holds back, opened in sentence one and paid in the final 15
  percent. No promises stack, refrains, chapters or mid-video turn.
- Map: hook (0-10) → a tight chronology that rises once (10-80) → the reveal that repays the
  opening (80-95) → a one-line maxim, then stop. Nothing widens out and there is no verdict block.
- A timestamp each 15-30 s; a retention map of 3-5 lines; at most one plain outro line.
- Vertical Shorts: the hook also works muted (assume on-screen text); a loop-close, where the last
  line runs back into the first, is worth trying.

## Output format

```
# <Working title> - <lane>, ~<N> min

## Hook options (pick one)
### Hook 1 - <type>
### Hook 2 - <type>
### Hook 3 - <type>
(each 40-80 words)

## Script
[0:00] narration as spoken text; punch sentences set apart
[ARCHIVE: ...] [B-ROLL: ...] [MAP: ...] [COUNTER: 118 left]
[VERIFY: a fact the customer must confirm]

## Retention map
[m:ss] a line for each promise, refrain, partial reveal, counter beat or cold-open repayment
```

Titles and headings never rate the script's own pacing or rule keeping; the retention map names
devices, not grades.
