## Hook types (fifteen)

| # | Type | Mechanics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ranked against the archive | The channel's own back catalogue is the yardstick; this case ranks high on it. Only with a real count. |
| 2 | Warning as tease | The warning names what is graphic and that it comes at the end: a promise with a location. |
| 3 | Found artifact | Something was recovered (a video, a photo), and its meaning is teased; the script commits to showing it near the end. |
| 4 | Someone I know | The narrator's real link to the case, or a sceptic at home who could not stop asking. Only true links the user gives. |
| 5 | Credibility reversal (paranormal) | Stories like this are never believable, except this one, because of who witnessed or studied it. |
| 6 | Genre tag | Name the sub-type ("a classic stay-out-of-the-woods story"). |
| 7 | Perfect surface | An enviable family or life, until the year someone close had a secret. |
| 8 | Scene, then rewind | One frightening moment, cut at its height, then the ritual, then the story from its start. |
| 9 | Unlike any other | What makes the case unique: an unusual volume of public footage, or a puzzling mind rather than a puzzling crime. |
| 10 | Raw audio first | An unnarrated emergency or jail call; the voice is identified afterwards. Only released audio. |
| 11 | The last ordinary minute | An exact clock time, a routine act, and no return; precise time stamps signal documentary authority. |
| 12 | Concept first | Teach one idea from forensics or psychology (how alibis get built, the trap of hindsight),
then test the case against it; the video reads as a lesson. |
| 13 | Damning stack | A list of the culprit's failures that ends on one absurd, exact figure. |
| 14 | Ritual and request | The series' own fixed incantation plus "you kept asking for this one"; the tease waits until after the intro. |
| 15 | Rabbit hole | The host's late-night descent into the case, with the viewer invited along. |

## Beat maps by lane

**Narrated story (usually 20-35 minutes):**
- First 45 s: the hook (any of types 1-8) with the warning.
- Up to about 1:30: the fixed ritual: a running joke about the like button, the subscribe line, a boundary
  phrase that says "here is the story", and a music sting. The boundary never changes, so the
  sting comes to mean the story is starting.
- About 3-85 percent: one continuous chronology: (a) ordinary life, 10-20 percent of runtime; (b)
  the anomaly, seen by whoever noticed it (a courier who sees the same parcel on the step three days
  in a row); (c) the investigation as a chain of leads and dead ends; (d) the suspect named; (e)
  **the second telling**: what investigators now believe happened, the whole crime retold from the
  inside with what the first telling withheld; (f) outcomes in three to five tight sentences.
- Halfway: one optional pause where the story has levelled off; nothing else breaks it.
- Final 60-90 s: the outro (the same joke word for word, platforms, the case-suggestion community).
- Compilations: three to five short cases, each shaped as a complete arc, ordered from least to most
  disturbing.

**Footage analysis (about 15-120 min):**
- 0-2: cold open (a thesis, emergency audio, or the last ordinary minute).
- 2-8: a condensed lead-up, flagged as condensed. The crime and the culprit are fully disclosed,
  with the method and the likely motive; the viewer is never asked to guess the culprit.
- 8-75: the main body: interview, bodycam and court footage in date order with analytic voiceover,
  arranged as encounters that keep getting tougher (first account, a second account, then the
  interview as it moves from warmth to exhaustion to challenge, then the arrest). Each police technique is named just before the viewer sees it
  work.
- 75-95: trial extracts: openings, one crushing cross-examination (an exact gap between two
  automated logs that breaks the alibi), closings.
- Last 1-2: the verdict on screen and one flat status line (when parole eligibility falls). No
  outro, no call to action, no summary.

**Conversational (about 30-80 min):**
- 0-2 min: the ritual intro (a theme-line bit, the series explained for newcomers, small talk).
- 2-4 min: light banter, closing on thanks to the audience.
- A disclaimer beat (close the video if it is too heavy).
- About 10-90 percent: the culprit's life (or the family's, in a mystery) from childhood to
  sentence: growing up, the first red flags, things getting worse, the crimes, the close calls, the capture, the
  court case, the sentence. The first killing often comes only 20-25 percent in; the capture sits at 80-85 percent
  and is told with open relief.
- Last 5-10 percent: an opinion segment, remembrance of the victims, a check on the side activity,
  the sign-off, a pointer to next week.

**The strategic split:** the narrated story holds back who and how until the last act; footage
analysis reveals who at once and runs on how the lie falls apart; the conversational lane puts who
in the title and runs on how they nearly escaped or were caught. All three obey one rule: suspense never hangs on what the title has already said.

## Retention devices

- **Promises with an address:** the hook says where the payoff sits; leaving early means losing a
  specific thing.
- **Engineered gaps:** neutral labels ("a figure in a grey coat on the petrol-station camera") held
  for many minutes before the reveal; the police's wrong theory told straight so its collapse lands
  as a turn.
- **Reframing turns:** a resolved beat, then "it would later emerge that ...", then something that
  widens the case.
- **Chapter foreshadowing:** every few minutes, a time marker and an unnamed shift.
- **Slow motion at the climax:** the payoff scene alone moves step by step in physical detail;
  everywhere else, time compresses.
- **Tactic loops:** notes like "a behaviour we will come back to"; riddles opened before they are
  solved ("many of you will have noticed one detail"); countable sub-loops ("he asks this nine times
  in the interview").
- **Precision as payoff:** an exact quantity held, then fired at the right moment (the exact time to
  the suspect's first real emotion; the gap that breaks the timeline).
- **Evidence the suspect does not know about:** tell the viewer early, then make them wait to watch
  the suspect confronted with it. Dramatic irony is the main loop.
- **Conversational cliffhangers:** "and this is the bit that got me"; mock suspense punctured for a
  laugh; the side activity itself as a progress bar that finishes with the story.

## Payoff rhythm

- **Narrated story:** long fuse, one explosion. Most of the runtime builds calmly with a small
  payoff every 2-4 minutes; the one big payoff sits at 80-90 percent; the aftermath is brisk and
  cool. After the climax, step straight back to timelines and evidence.
- **Footage analysis:** a ladder rather than one explosion, a rung every 5-10 minutes. A full
  confession is often never given; the verdict text closes it. Light moments are spacers between
  heavy segments.
- **Conversational:** an even tempo, with the host landing a quip each 20-40 s; escalation by
  arithmetic (victims, money); the arrest is the moment of release, around 80-85 percent, the verdict
  quick, then a short opinion outro that releases the built-up anger.

Across the genre: atrocity is never placed next to atrocity. Each lane interleaves it with
procedure, analysis or (conversational only) jokes and craft.

## Ethics and tone

- **Warnings are contracts:** a mature-audience label plus a spoken re-warning with the payoff's
  place; in conversational episodes, reminders mid-video and permission to leave.
- **Personhood before peril:** minutes on the victims' lives before anything goes wrong; their hopes
  named; in footage analysis, the opening scene belongs to the victim. Withholding a name, and
  saying why on camera, builds trust.
- **Speculation fenced:** "the full file was never released, so this is careful inference";
  "allegedly"; "pleaded not guilty and awaits trial"; graded confidence ("it is safe to assume";
  "the broad view is"); opinion loudly flagged; each claim tied to who said it.
- **No glamour, on camera:** criticise killer fandom; mock the performer, never the victims; show
  killers as small and pathetic.
- **Institutional failure named:** where the record shows police or agencies failed, say so.
- **Gore narrated, not lingered on:** no post-mortem images in frame; mechanism briefly, then
  consequence; nervous laughter named for what it is.

## Quick card

- **Hooks (45 s or less):** ranked against the archive; warning with an address; found artifact;
  last ordinary minute; unlike any other; perfect surface; scene then rewind.
- **One case (20-45 min):** 0-2 hook and warning → 2-5 ritual ending on the same sting → 5-20
  ordinary life (victim first if the culprit is hidden; culprit first if named) → 20-45 anomaly and
  investigation through the discoverers' eyes, a lead every 2-4 minutes, the wrong theory told
  straight → 45-55 optional act break, never later → 55-80 rising stakes, segments closing on a contradiction, a half-reveal or a later-emerged turn → 80-90 the payoff, time slowed → 90-97
  outcomes, one exact figure → 97-100 the same outro ritual or a cold verdict card; no recap.
- **Rules:** spoken register; past for background, present at discovery; address the viewer once an
  act; time marker plus unnamed shift; neutral labels until the reveal; exact numbers early, the
  lie meets them late; horror never beside horror; warnings, hedges and named omissions said
  aloud; the mystery run is never the title's; resolve inside the video.
