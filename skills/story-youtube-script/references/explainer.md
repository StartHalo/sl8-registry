## Intake

Fill gaps with the defaults below and name them in the delivery (HR12: no question tool).

1. **Lane**, which sets the skeleton:
   - **Host-led** (on camera): builds, experiments, investigations; what is at risk is the host's
     body, money or pride.
   - **Voiceover essay** over visuals or animation: science, economics, the story of a company or a
     country; the densest wording.
   - **Inquiry-led** (host or voiceover): one question is the main character and the video works
     like a detective case.
   Default: voiceover essay; host-led only when the brief says the host performs a real build or
   test; a kit or brief may name another.
2. **The one question.** An explainer promises to answer exactly one. Turn a bare topic into a
   question with tension (for "solar sails": "how does light, which weighs nothing, push a ship?").
3. **A wrong belief** (optional): something viewers are sure of that is false. Reversing it primes
   retention better than anything else.
4. **Length.** About 140-150 spoken words a minute; voiceover essays run denser. Default: 10
   minutes. At 4 minutes or less, use Short-form mode.
5. **The concrete anchor.** A single thing (an object, a demonstration, a figure, a scene) that
   the script returns to again and again.
   If the brief has no anchor, build the analogy object under "Analogy method".

## Hooks

Three options from different rows, each 30 s or less and 40-80 words. Each does two jobs: it puts a concrete picture in the viewer's head and it raises a question the
script is then obliged to settle. Hooks sound
sure of themselves; hedging belongs in the middle, never here.

| Type | Shape (our wording) |
|---|---|
| Object | Point at one thing, give it a sharp figure or record, then the catch or the question. |
| Misconception reversal | The thing everyone has heard, declared wrong, and the question of what is really happening. |
| What-if | A vivid imagined scenario, the disaster it surely causes, and a "not quite". |
| Impossible thing | Something record-breaking that exists and breaks a rule the viewer relies on. |
| Mid-climax | The host inside the peak moment, then a jump back months to show how it came to this. |
| Casualty puzzle | A year, a place, specific losses, and nobody understood why. |
| Dare | The viewer cannot do something, or should look away now. |

Optional boosts: a quick (about 15 s) run of news clips straight after the hook, to show the topic
is real and argued over; always sharp numbers rather than round ones ("1,388", not "about 1,400").

## Contract, then beats

**Contract (0:30-1:30):** show how the subject touches the viewer personally, then list the
questions, ideally three, to be answered in that order. Listing them keeps people watching, since
every question is a loop whose answer has been promised by name.

**Beats, as shares of the runtime:**
- **12-30, groundwork:** explain only as much of the mechanism as the rest needs, through one anchor analogy (method below).
- **30-70 Ladder:** run the same mechanism at growing scale or across a series of cases, each rung
  using the words the last one taught. A "but" turn at least every 90 s.
- **60-75, the twist:** the hook's promised reversal arrives. A false ending is worth considering: write the
  wrong conclusion out in full, even "The End", then knock it down.
- **85-95 Wider view:** reframe the topic in terms of humanity, deep time, or who the viewer is. The close delivers meaning; the most surprising fact was already given at about 70 percent.
- **Last 5:** if wanted, one final sentence (a further question to chase, or a plain subscribe
  line); otherwise stop on the wider view.

**Lane variants:**
- *Host-led:* a quest told in order (the dream → failed attempts, counted → test day → payoff), with the heavy science kept back
until the viewer is invested, and asked for openly ("stay with me for the technical bit").
- *Inquiry-led:* clues come in the sequence they were uncovered, with pauses that ask how we can
  be sure ("hang on, how do we actually know that?").
- *Voiceover essay:* a chain of causes, one link a paragraph, with the twist around the fifth link.

## Analogy method (required for abstract ideas)

1. **Signal it:** "picture ..." or "it works like ...".
2. **Keep to the everyday object's rules** for two to four sentences with no jargon (a black
   hole's edge as a travelator faster than anyone can sprint: talk only about the belt, how fast
   it goes, how fast you go, and the spot past which you cannot get back).
3. **Come back with the technical term attached.**
4. **From then on, use it as a shortcut** whenever the idea comes back.
5. **Say where it breaks down;** owning the limit earns trust for the next analogy.

Also: give unseen agents feelings and motives (cells that panic and shout); pair each big figure with an everyday yardstick (buses, mountains, wallets, marathons); calibrate a sensation by naming a
familiar one and then correcting it.

## Register and retention

- **Address "you" by default;** use "I" only as a fellow learner, at most one step ahead. Owning up to not knowing is what earns the right to explain.
- Present tense, history included: give the date, then narrate as if live. Use contractions
  throughout.
- Rhythm: let a run of building clauses land on a blunt verdict of three to six words. The short
  sentence is the moment of understanding.
- **Jargon order:** show the thing, then name it, then defuse the name within a sentence with a
  homely image or a joke.
- At least one reveal, twist or laugh in every 90 s.
- Every promise is paid inside the same act ("we'll come back to that" has a due date); close every
  loop on screen.
- Call back only to what this script set up; never "remember X from earlier" without the earlier
  line.
- If something can be demonstrated, show the demonstration rather than quoting a source (only a
  test the user really ran or the source documents).
- Hedge plainly in the middle ("honestly, nobody knows yet"), never in the hook.
- Before correcting a false belief, show why it was a sensible thing to think; nobody should feel
  foolish.

## Ending

No invented sponsor, brand or plug. A specific integration the user asks for stays outside the
ladder, the turn and the wider view. Otherwise the wider view is the ending: reframe, then stop.
A single plain sentence that keeps the curiosity going (a further question, or an invitation to
keep following) is optional, and never begs.

## Anti-patterns

- Opening on a definition: a definition comes only once there is a tension it resolves.
- "Today we're going to talk about": let the viewer see the subject; do not announce it.
- Lecture register: "as we can see", "it is important to note", "in conclusion".
- More than about 30 s of abstraction with no physical anchor, measured number or joke.
- Unpaid promises, moralising before the mechanism, round numbers where exact ones exist.
- Invented monetization; press-release register anywhere.

## Short-form mode

At 4 minutes or less, drop the long-form map; keep the viewer as main character, show then name,
and no bare numbers.
- About 150 words a minute, so 3 minutes holds roughly 420-470 spoken words.
- A single question, a single analogy and a single twist. Reversing a false belief makes the best
  short hook: line
  one states the wrong belief, line two breaks it.
- Skip the list of questions; with only one question, asking it is the whole promise.
- Map: hook (0-10) → the mechanism told through its anchor analogy (10-60) → the twist (60-85) → payoff
  and a one-sentence wider view, then stop.
- A single loop, raised in sentence one and settled in the last. No promises, false ending or ladder
  beyond one rung.
- A timestamp every 15-30 s; a 3-5 line retention map; at most one plain outro line.
- Vertical Shorts: the concrete picture on screen within 2 s; the hook works muted; a loop-close is
  worth trying.

## Output format

```
# <Working title> - <lane>, ~<N> min

## Hook options (pick one)
### Hook 1 - <type>
### Hook 2 - <type>
### Hook 3 - <type>
(each 40-80 words)

## Script
[0:00] spoken text; blunt verdict sentences set apart
[ANIMATION: ...] [DEMO: ...] [B-ROLL: ...] [ON-SCREEN: 1,388]

## Retention map
[m:ss] a line for each contract question, promise, "but" turn or false ending
```

Titles and headings never rate the script's own pacing or rule keeping; the retention map names
devices, not grades.
